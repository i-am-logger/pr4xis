## 2. What we see, and why this tool addresses it

Families arrive at our door with questions we are not always able to answer:
whether a Medicaid waiver covers what they need, what the difference is between
skilled and custodial care, what a level-of-care evaluation means, what happens to
their family member's eligibility if circumstances change. These are questions
with real answers written in federal law and program rules — but those answers are
scattered, written for administrators rather than families, and hard to find at
the moment a family actually needs them.

What families get instead is a mix of guesswork, out-of-date advice from other
families, and increasingly, confident answers from general-purpose AI tools that
are sometimes simply wrong. In our setting a wrong answer is not harmless. A
family that acts on a bad answer about an enrollment window or an eligibility rule
can lose access to services for months.

I have reviewed the pr4xis Caregiver Answer Engine. In plain terms, what it does
is this:

- It answers a caregiver's plain-language question about caregiving programs and
  terminology, and **every answer it gives carries the actual statute, regulation,
  or official federal guidance it came from**, so the family can see the source
  rather than take the tool's word for it.
- When it does not hold a reliable, cited answer, **it says so and points to a
  human channel** rather than producing something plausible. This is the part that
  matters most to me. It is the opposite of how the general-purpose tools our
  families are already using behave.
- It runs entirely inside the family's own web browser. **There is no account, no
  sign-in, and the question does not leave their device** — which matters when
  someone is asking about a diagnosis or a family member's capacity.
- There is no cost per use and no server to run, so a small organization like ours
  could put it in front of families without a budget line or an IT project.

I am not endorsing it as a finished product, and its authors have not asked me to.
They have been direct with me about what it does not yet do. I am supporting it
because the design decision at its center — refuse rather than guess, and show the
source — is the right one for the families I serve.

## Notes for you, not part of the letter

**Why the specifics matter.** ACL's Partnerships criterion asks two things: whether
collaboration runs *throughout the process*, and whether the project has identified
stakeholders who could support it — naming *design consultation*, *operations and
maintenance*, *workforce training*, and *AI education* as qualifying purposes.
Section 3 above is written to answer that directly. A letter that only says "I
support this project" answers neither question.

**The access is worth more than the letter.** ACL's User-Centered criterion asks
what caregiver input shaped the design. Right now the honest answer in our
application is *none* — no caregiver has used this tool. Commitment #1 above is
what changes that, and it is the single most valuable thing in this letter.

**What we will not do.** We will not describe you as a partner, a collaborator, or
a supporter in any way beyond what you write and sign here. If you sign a letter
committing to two things, the application will say you committed to two things.
